The Jobs Came Back. Did Every Industry?

Plotly
labor market
interactive visualization
An interactive payroll chart shows what the national recovery date hides about eleven major sectors.
Author

Junzheng Yang

Published

October 25, 2026

“Jobs are back” sounds like a finish line. But a national total can recover while particular industries still employ fewer people. When U.S. payroll employment regained its February 2020 level, had every major sector recovered too? That distinction matters when judging what a recovery headline tells someone looking for work in a particular part of the economy.

The answer is no. In this data snapshot, total nonfarm payrolls first returned to their starting level in June 2022, yet 5 of the eleven major sectors remained below theirs. The interactive chart lets you inspect those differences rather than treating the national line as everyone’s experience.

Explore the recovery

The data are monthly, seasonally adjusted estimates from the Bureau of Labor Statistics payroll survey, obtained through FRED. The chart follows February 2020 through December 2024, using eleven broad sectors, including government, alongside the national total. Each percentage line starts at zero because it compares a sector with its own February 2020 payroll count. Seasonal adjustment reduces recurring calendar effects, making month-to-month comparisons more useful.

Try this: hover or tap a line for its date, payroll count, and both measures of change. Click legend entries to show or hide sectors; double-click one to isolate it. The dropdown restores the recovery comparison, selects goods-producing sectors, or displays every sector. Switch between % change and Job change; the latter axis is in thousands. Drag the date slider to inspect a shorter period, or drag a box within the plot to zoom. Double-click the plot to reset the zoom.

Monthly payroll employment relative to February 2020. The dotted vertical line marks the national total’s first return to baseline in this snapshot. Source: BLS Current Employment Statistics via FRED. Downloaded October 25, 2026. Open the chart separately or download the plotted data.

A national crossing, several unfinished recoveries

Start with the default comparison and hover around the vertical line. Professional and business services stood 5.0% above its starting level. Leisure and hospitality was still 6.5% below, representing 1,095,000 fewer jobs. Private education and health services, other services, mining and logging, and government were also below baseline. Gains elsewhere were sufficient to offset these remaining shortfalls in the national total.

This is why a recovery date needs a clearly defined unit. It marks when a total crosses a threshold; it does not establish that the same workers found new jobs, that vacancies were plentiful, or that wages recovered. Payroll statistics count jobs rather than unique people: someone holding two payroll jobs can be counted twice, as BLS explains.

The units change the comparison

Now compare mining and logging with private education and health services near the end of 2024. Mining was 9.1% below baseline, while private education and health was 9.7% above. Those percentages have similar magnitudes. Switching to Job change reveals very different scales: about 62,000 jobs lost versus 2,372,000 gained. Percentage changes describe the size of a shift relative to a sector; job counts describe its numerical contribution to the national change. Neither answers both questions.

Select Goods-producing sectors and follow manufacturing after its first return above zero. By December 2024 it was slightly below baseline again. Returning once is not the same as remaining above the threshold. Across all sectors, 4 ended the window below baseline, although 3 of those gaps were less than one percent. These small endpoint differences should not be described as evidence of a large collapse.

These are revised historical estimates downloaded in 2026, not the numbers available to journalists in 2022. BLS revises payroll estimates, so crossing dates can change. The chart does not identify why sectors diverged, and returning to 2020 employment is not equivalent to catching up with population growth or a pre-pandemic trend. The takeaway: the national total recovered before every sector did. Check the sector, date, and units before interpreting what “jobs are back” means.
